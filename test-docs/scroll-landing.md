# Lighthouse keeper's handbook, Cawdle Point (scroll landing stress document)

A stress document for table-of-contents landing, dressed as a handbook: the first sections are short and the later ones are long lists, the shape that defeats lazy height estimates. Open it fresh, click "6. Open questions" in the table of contents, and check where the viewer lands.

## 1. Overview

Cawdle Point Light stands on the last rock of Cawdle Point, where Pellock Sound opens to the sea and the Grindles reef waits for anyone who cuts the corner. It shows two white flashes every fifteen seconds from 38 metres above high water, and in fog it sounds two blasts a minute from a machine the village calls Mother.

This handbook is for the next keeper. It assumes you can trim a wick and read a barometer; it does not assume you know where anything is, why there is a chair in the lamp room, or what the gull wants.

## 2. Where the rules come from

Nearly every rule here began as a line in the Daybook: something went wrong, the keeper wrote down what, and the next keeper wrote down what not to do about it. Absalom Trigg, the first keeper, had collected 212 rules by the time he retired in 1902, four of them about hats.

In 1923 the Pellock Sound Lights Committee boiled the 212 down to three invariants and a great deal of advice. The invariants follow; the advice is the rest of this handbook; and when the advice runs out, the Daybook is still on the shelf by the barometer.

## 3. The three invariants

Everything else in this handbook can be bargained with, by the weather, the relief boat or the Committee. These cannot.

- **The light burns from sunset to sunrise**: Every night, whatever the weather, the boat or the kettle is doing.
- **The character never changes**: Two white flashes every fifteen seconds, because a ship that sees the wrong light is worse off than one that sees none.
- **The log is written as it happens**: Not at the end of the watch, not over breakfast, and never from memory.
- **Tea**: Not an invariant, whatever the relief crews tell you, although Trigg's thirty-day rule has never once been broken.

## 4. Duties by season

The Point has four seasons -- before the first storm, the storms, the fog, and one fortnight in July when nothing happens and the Committee visits -- so after the first list this section gives up on the calendar and goes room by room.

### 4a. Before the first storm

- **Walk the gallery rail**: In the first week of September, walk the gallery with a mallet and tap all 48 stanchions. A sound one rings and a rusting one says "thock". Chalk the ones that thock and write them in the log as well, because the chalk will be gone by morning.
- **Clear the gutters**: The tower sheds rain like a church roof, and the cottage gutters take all of it. Clear them after the last leaves have blown off the headland, which is later than you think, and again after the first gale, which is sooner. A blocked downpipe on the seaward corner empties straight into the oil store, and paraffin is not improved by rain.
- **Haul up the dinghy**: From October to April the dinghy lives above the spring-tide mark, lashed to the two rings by the slip. It has been lost twice, both times by keepers who knew the sea would never come that far up. The rings were set by Trigg, who did not know either, and set them anyway.
- **Fit the storm shutters**: The cottage has eight windows and eight shutters, and they are not interchangeable, whatever the painted numbers say. Fit them in daylight, on a still day, with somebody holding the other end. Shutter five fits window six and has done since 1953; nobody has had the heart to repaint it.
- **Fill the paraffin tank**: Top the main tank to the brim and check the reserve drums against the stores ledger. Dip the tank with the brass rod rather than trusting the gauge, which is decorative. The ninety-day winter reserve is not a suggestion; it was paid for in 1911.
- **Test the telephone**: Ring the harbourmaster and ask for the time. The line works in all weathers except the ones you need it for, so learn the flag signals as well (section 4e). If the harbourmaster is out, the bakery will tell you the time, the weather and anything else you let it.
- **Take in the washing line**: The line comes down in October. It is not a safety item until the first gale, and after that it is the only one anybody mentions. The pegs live in the tin on the kitchen windowsill, out of reach of every gull but one.
- **Check the barometer**: Tap it, read it, and compare it with the harbour glass over the telephone. Ours reads a tenth of an inch low and always has; do not correct it, because every entry in the Daybook assumes it. A fall of a tenth in an hour means shutters, and two tenths means shutters and the telephone.
- **Move the hens**: The hen house goes into the lee of the oil store for the winter. The hens do not approve, and they will say so every morning until March. Lay the straw thick and the complaints will be shorter.

### 4b. Lamp room

- **Light up by the almanac**: Lighting-up time is fifteen minutes before sunset by the almanac, not by the window. The window is the least reliable instrument on the Point, and the first rule in the Daybook says so. The almanac hangs on the nail inside the lamp room door, where the window cannot see it.
- **Draw the curtains at sunrise**: The lens will focus the morning sun as happily as it spreads the lamp, and it has set fire to two logbooks and a hat. The curtains go round the lens before you go down to breakfast, every day, cloudy ones included.
- **Wind the clockwork**: The weight takes two hours and ten minutes to fall the length of the tower, so wind it every two hours and never later. If the lens stops, the light shows fixed and white, and a fixed white light off the Grindles says "harbour" to anyone who has not read the chart.
- **Look at the mantle twice**: Check the vapour burner's mantle at the start of every watch, from the side as well as the front. A mantle can look whole and be cracked, and it will choose the worst night of the year to prove it. Change it in daylight if you can; fitting one in the dark is a job for three hands.
- **Clean the lens with spirit and linen**: Linen only, spirit only, bare hands never. The lens is older than anything else on the Point, the tower included, and it behaves accordingly. Work from the top panel down, and do not breathe on the prisms, however much it seems to help.
- **Keep the floor clear**: Nothing lives on the lamp room floor: no buckets, no boots, no books. Everything dropped up here rolls towards the stairs, and the stairs are 117 steps long. The last bucket to try it reached the kitchen at half past two in the morning, by way of every step.
- **Leave the chair alone**: There is one chair in the lamp room and it is not for sitting in. Nobody remembers why it is there, but every keeper who has moved it has logged a bad night, and the Daybook has the dates.
- **Wipe the storm panes**: Condensation forms on the inside and salt on the outside, and only one of them wipes off from in here. Do the gallery side in the morning, clipped on, and never in a wind you have to lean into. Anything that needs both hands can wait for a calmer day, and nearly everything on the gallery needs both hands.
- **Check the pressure on the half hour**: The vapour burner wants its pressure steady, not high. Pump gently, write the reading in the log, and remember that a light that roars is a light that is about to go out. The gauge sticks in cold weather, so tap it once, politely, before believing it.
- **Test the standby monthly**: On the first of every month, run the electric standby lamp for ten minutes with the main burner out. Warn the cottage first; the generator and the kettle have never agreed about the fuses. Log the test, and the kettle.
- **Carry up only what you can carry down**: Everything you take up the stairs has to come down again in the dark, in a hurry, with one hand on the rail. Plan accordingly, and leave the tea tray at the bottom. Tea is taken at the foot of the tower, where there is a chair you are allowed to sit in.

### 4c. Fog signal

- **Start Mother by the buoy**: Start the fog signal when you can no longer see the Grindles buoy, not when you can no longer see the pier head. By the time the pier head goes, the fog has been sitting on the reef for twenty minutes. At night, watch the buoy's light and start when you miss two of its flashes in a row.
- **Give the compressor its twenty minutes**: Mother's compressor needs twenty minutes to build pressure from cold, and nothing hurries it. If you are wondering whether to start the engine, start the engine. The log records the minute you started, not the minute you meant to.
- **Two blasts a minute, no more**: Mother's character is two blasts of three seconds every sixty seconds. It is printed in the notices to mariners and, like the light's, it does not change for anybody. The fishermen count her in fog the way they count the light at night, and they will know within the hour if you have been creative.
- **Ring the harbour**: When Mother starts, ring the harbourmaster, who rings the bakery, who puts the second batch in. The village has baked to the fog signal since 1888 and sees no reason to stop. When the telephone is out, the bakery works it out for itself, usually before the harbour does.
- **Wear the ear defenders**: The ear defenders hang on the engine house door. Put them on before you open it, because the doorway is where Mother is loudest. Keeper Trigg had none, and spent his last fourteen years as keeper asking everybody to repeat themselves.
- **Log every start and stop**: Each start and each stop goes in the log and the fog register, with the time and the visibility. The harbour keeps a copy of the register, the two are compared at the end of the year, and the Committee enjoys a discrepancy.
- **Run on after the fog lifts**: When the buoy reappears, keep Mother going for ten more minutes. Fog on the Sound lifts from the Point first and from the reef last, and a boat still inside it cannot see that you can see. Ten minutes of noise is cheaper than a boat on the Grindles.
- **Count the echo**: The cliff behind the cottage returns each blast about two seconds late. If you hear three blasts, one of them is the cliff; if you hear four, go and look at the engine. Four has happened twice, and both times it was the belt.
- **No practice after nine**: Practice blasts are not sounded after nine in the evening, a rule the Committee made in 1952 after a summer tenant complained. The tenant left in October; the rule stayed. Practise on Saturday mornings instead, briefly, when the village expects it.
- **Watch for the Porridge**: Summer fog comes in from the south-west in a low, thick bank the village calls the Porridge. It can sit on the Point while the harbour is in sunshine, so trust the buoy, not the telephone. It smells faintly of seaweed and arrives, more often than seems fair, on a Tuesday (section 6).
- **Keep a spare drive belt**: There is a spare belt on the nail by the engine house door and another in the stores. When the first one breaks you will need the second quickly and the third eventually. Order the next one on the day you use one.
- **Grease the piston monthly**: The diaphone piston wants a thin film of grease, not a thick one. A thick one makes Mother sound as if she has a cold, and the fishermen will tell you so. Use the tin marked PISTON, not the tin marked HINGES, even though they hold the same grease.
- **Mind the cows**: The farm on the headland moves its cows to the top field whenever Mother is due for maintenance. The cows have never forgiven 1888, and in fairness neither has the farm. Ring the farm the day before; forget, and the cows will move themselves, usually through the fence.
- **Stop in the right order**: Shut the air valve first and the engine second, never the other way round. Done backwards, Mother lets out a long, low note that brings the village to its windows and the harbourmaster to the telephone. The Daybook records the date on which each keeper learned this.

### 4d. Supplies and stores

- **Paraffin, ninety days**: Keep ninety days of paraffin on the Point from October to April and sixty for the rest of the year. The relief boat has been nineteen days late before and will be again. The drums lie on their sides in the oil store and are rolled a quarter turn on the first of each month, which keeps the seals wet and the keeper honest.
- **Mantles, twice what you think**: Order twice as many mantles as the stores ledger says you use. They break in the post, in the box, and occasionally from being looked at. Keep them in the tin on the top shelf of the lamp room store, away from damp, hens and anyone in a hurry.
- **Tea, thirty days**: Trigg's rule, and the most quoted line in the Daybook. It is advice rather than an invariant, but no keeper has cared to test the difference. Count the tea when you count the paraffin, and trust the tea count more.
- **Water**: The cistern holds about six weeks of water at the usual rate, and three if there are visitors. Boil it from August, when it starts to taste of the roof. The gutters feed it (section 4a), so a gutter cleared in October is a clean cup of tea in December.
- **Coal for the range**: The range takes a sack every three days in winter. The coal lives in the bunker by the back door, not in the oil store, however much tidier that would look. Coal dust and paraffin together make a smell that the cottage keeps until spring.
- **Spare glass**: Two spare lamp chimneys and one spare storm pane live wrapped in the blanket marked GLASS. The blanket is not a blanket and is not to be used as one, even in weather like 1953. Hold the pane up to the lamp each October to look for cracks, carefully, with both hands.
- **Log books**: Two blank log books wait in the dry box under the stairs. Running out of log book is the only way to break the third invariant through no fault of your own. When you start one, order another on the next boat, so that there are always two.
- **Clock spring**: A spare mainspring for the clockwork hangs in the oil store in a greased cloth. It has never been needed, and every keeper has checked it at least once a year regardless. Should it ever be wanted, the fitting instructions are in the Daybook for 1879, in Coggin's hand.
- **Medical box**: The medical box is on the kitchen wall, stocked for burns, cuts and falls, in roughly that order. Replace what you use on the next boat, and write down what you used it for. The Committee reads the second list with more interest than the first.
- **Rope**: Keep one new coil and one old. The new coil is for the boat and the old one for everything else, and it is always the new one that gets used. When the old one finally goes, the new one becomes the old one, and nobody is fooled.
- **The boots**: A pair of size twelve sea boots stands at the back of the oil store and appears in the ledger as "boots, one pair, spare". Nobody knows whose they are; leave them where they are, and count them. They are dusted once a year, in July, before the inspector arrives.
- **Stocktaking**: The Committee's inspector counts the stores every July, spoons included. There have been forty-one spoons since 1923 and he checks every one, so do not lend any to the relief boat. He arrives on one boat, leaves on the next, and eats with the spoons he is counting.

### 4e. Visitors and boats

- **The relief boat**: The *Patience* comes every second Thursday, weather permitting, with Dorcas Hebble at the wheel and the post in a biscuit tin. If she says the weather is turning, believe her before the barometer does, and have everything at the slip in ten minutes.
- **Landing**: Boats land at the slip at half tide or above, and never in an easterly. The slip is steeper than it looks and wetter than it seems, and it has the scars of both. Wait for the skipper's nod before stepping ashore, and put the post ashore before yourself.
- **Flag signals**: When the telephone is out, one white flag on the gallery means all well, two mean send the boat, and a red one means send the boat and the doctor. The village has never seen the red flag, and we would like to keep it that way.
- **Signing in**: Every visitor signs the visitors' book, the Committee, the doctor and the harbourmaster included. It is the only record of who was on the Point when something went missing. The last thing to go missing was a spoon, which came back fifteen years later in a different pattern.
- **The tower on sunny days**: Visitors go up the tower only with the lens curtains drawn. The lens will focus the sun through a visitor's hat as readily as through a logbook, and it has done both. In summer the curtains stay closed from ten until four, whatever anyone wants to photograph.
- **The school visit**: The village school comes once a year in June and asks the same three questions: how many steps, whether it is haunted, and what the gull is called. The answers are 117, no, and the Admiral.
- **The painter**: A painter from inland comes every August and paints the tower from the cove. Be polite, and do not mention that the red band in the picture is level (section 6). The pictures sell well in the village, and one hangs in the harbour office, band and all.
- **Boats in the cove**: Fishing boats may shelter in the cove in a westerly without asking. Take them a pot of tea if the weather allows, and note the time they arrived for the log. They usually leave a fish on the slip, which the Admiral regards as his.

### 4f. Repairs and changes

- **Write it down first**: Nothing in the lamp room is changed until the change is in the log. Write what you mean to do, do it, and then write what you actually did.
- **Repaint every third year**: The tower is whitewashed every third summer and the red band repainted with it. Use the band marks cut in the stone on the landward side, not your eye, and not the painter's picture.
- **Step 88**: Step 88 has moved since the storm of 1953 and may not be repaired without the Committee's approval. Step over it, and tell every visitor to do the same.
- **Do not improve Mother**: Every keeper has had an idea for making the fog signal quieter, louder or more musical. The fishermen know her voice as it is, and that is the only improvement that matters.
- **Report to the Committee**: Changes are reported to the Pellock Sound Lights Committee on the green form. They reply within six to eight weeks, usually to ask for the green form.
- **Keep the old part**: Anything replaced goes in the crate under the stairs with a label and a date. About once a decade, one of them turns out to be the better part.

## 6. Open questions

- **Who winds the kitchen clock?** No keeper in living memory has wound the kitchen clock, and it keeps better time than the almanac. It stopped once, for forty minutes on a March night in 1937, and then started again on its own. The Daybook entry for that night says only "clock", underlined twice.
- **What does the Admiral want?** Every evening at lighting-up time the herring gull known as the Admiral stands on stanchion 17 of the gallery rail, facing the reef. He has done so since 1974 and has outlasted two keepers. The present view is that he is supervising, and nobody has offered a better one.
- **Is the red band level?** The red band was repainted in 1938 and has sat four centimetres lower on the seaward side ever since, unless the tower leans, depending on whom you ask. The painter paints it level every August. The Committee regards this as either artistic licence or evidence, and has not said which.
- **Should Mother have a third blast?** The fishermen have asked for a third blast since 1920, on the grounds that two are easily lost in a swell. The Committee replies that the character never changes, which is true, and that the fishermen know it, which is also true. The request comes round again every spring with the lobster pots.
- **Whose boots are they?** The size twelve sea boots in the oil store appear in every stores ledger since 1923 and fit nobody who has lived on the Point. Keeper Trigg's feet were, by his own account, "neat". The inspector counts the boots every July and has never once asked.
- **What was on page 113?** Page 113 of the first Daybook has been cut out cleanly, between the entries for the third and fifth of March 1889. Trigg never mentioned it, and by the harbour's record the fourth was a calm day. Theories are welcome, and several have been written in the margin.
- **Why Tuesdays?** The fog register shows fog arriving on a Tuesday far more often than chance allows. Nobody has a theory worth writing down, and several have been written down anyway. The bakery has adjusted its Tuesday batch regardless.
- **Should the standby become the lamp?** The electric standby has been the standby since 1961 and has been used in earnest on eleven nights. The Committee has approved electrification four times and funded it never. Until it does, the vapour burner is the light and the standby is a promise.
- **Where does the Grindles buoy go?** The chart puts the buoy 1.4 nautical miles west-south-west of the tower, and on most days the buoy agrees. After a big westerly it drags a cable or so inshore, and within the week it is back on its own. Nobody has explained the coming back.
- **What is the chair for?** The chair in the lamp room has no known purpose, no known maker and no scratches, which for anything on the Point is remarkable. Trigg's first inventory lists it as "chair, lamp room, not to be sat in", and says nothing more. It is possible that the chair is the rule and the rule is the chair.
- **Who made the lens?** The crate the lens came in went into the range in the winter of 1874, and the maker's name went with it. The lens carries no mark we can find, and three polite letters abroad have come back unanswered. We would still like to say thank you.
- **Can the telephone be trusted?** The line to the harbour has failed in every storm on record and works perfectly on still days. The exchange says the fault is at our end, and the harbour says it is at the exchange. The flags have never failed, but they cannot tell you the time.
- **Is anyone else counting the spoons?** There were forty-one spoons in 1923 and there are forty-one now, which on a lighthouse is suspicious in itself. Two have been lost in that time and two have turned up, and neither pair matches the set. The inspector says the count is the count.

## 7. Incident log

1. **1871, first light**: Keeper Trigg lit the lamp for the first time on the first of November, eleven minutes late by the almanac, and wrote the first rule of the Daybook the same night. It concerns lighting-up time and the unreliability of windows.
2. **1874, the crate**: In a hard January the lens crate went into the range, maker's name and all. The rule that nothing is burned until somebody has read it dates from the following morning.
3. **1879, the fixed light**: Assistant Keeper Ned Coggin fell asleep on watch, the clockwork ran down, and the light showed fixed for forty minutes. A schooner took it for the harbour and anchored in the lee of the Grindles, by good luck in a calm. Coggin wound every two hours for the rest of his life, including, his wife said, in his sleep.
4. **1888, Mother arrives**: The fog signal was installed in May and first sounded in earnest in June. The cows on the headland gave no milk for a week, and the bakery began baking to her the same month.
5. **1895, the log from memory**: A relief keeper wrote up four days of the log at the end of the week and remembered a vessel passing north that had in fact gone aground to the south. The third invariant existed in spirit before 1923; this entry is what gave it teeth.
6. **1902, Trigg retires**: Keeper Trigg left the Point with 212 rules in the Daybook, a pension and a hearing trumpet. His last entry reads "Light burning, all well, tea low", which his successor took as an instruction.
7. **1911, the long January**: A gale held the relief boat in harbour for nineteen days, and the light burned on a lowered flame for the last six nights of it. The ninety-day reserve dates from that spring.
8. **1923, the consolidation**: The Committee reduced the 212 rules to three invariants and advice, and counted the spoons for the first time: forty-one. The four rules about hats were retired without comment.
9. **1938, the red band**: The tower was repainted over three weeks of good weather, and the red band came out four centimetres lower on the seaward side. The keeper of the day said it looked level from the Sound, and he may have been right.
10. **1952, the complaint**: A summer tenant at the old rectory complained about practice blasts after dark, in writing, twice. The Committee ruled that none be sounded after nine in the evening, and the second letter is framed in the engine house.
11. **1953, the great storm**: A January storm took three stanchions off the gallery rail, loosened step 88 and dragged the Grindles buoy half a mile inshore. The light burned all night on the keeper's hands and a great deal of rope, and the glass blanket was used as a blanket, once.
12. **1961, the standby lamp**: The electric standby was installed in March, and its first test blew the cottage fuses and, somehow, the kettle. Every monthly test since has begun with a warning to the cottage.

## 8. For the next keeper

You will be told that nothing happens at Cawdle Point, and for eleven months of the year that is true; this handbook is for the twelfth. Read it once now, and again on the night before your first winter.

- **Step 88**: Step over it, not on it, and do not let the Committee tell you it has been mended.
- **The Admiral**: Do not feed him; he has already decided that you will.
- **The Daybook**: Read one year of it every winter, and you will meet most of your troubles before they arrive.
- **The Patience**: If Dorcas Hebble says the weather is turning, it has already turned.
- **The chair**: Leave it where it is.
- **The light**: When in doubt, look up; if it is flashing twice every fifteen seconds, everything else can wait.

## 9. Sources

Everything in this handbook can be checked against the following, all kept on the Point unless noted.

- **The Daybook, first volume (1871-1902)**: Keeper Trigg's record of the light's first thirty-one years, and the source of all 212 rules. Page 113 is missing (section 6).
- **The Daybook, later volumes**: Eleven more volumes, one per keeper or per decade, whichever ran out first. They live on the shelf by the barometer and do not go up the tower.
- **Standing Orders of the Pellock Sound Lights Committee**: The 1923 consolidation that produced the three invariants, now in its fourth edition. The first three editions are kept for the arguments.
- **The log**: The watch-by-watch record the third invariant requires. It is written as things happen and read, mostly, by the Committee.
- **The stores ledger**: Every drum, mantle, spoon and pair of boots on the Point since 1923. It is the most accurate document we have and the least read.
- **The fog register**: Start and stop times for Mother since 1888, kept in duplicate with the harbour. The two copies have never agreed about a whole year, which is probably correct.
- **The visitors' book**: Every signature since the first landing, the Committee's included. Useful for settling who was on the Point, and for learning what the school thinks of the stairs.
- **Letters from the relief boat**: Notes left in the biscuit tin by successive skippers of the *Patience*, mostly about weather and now and then about the Admiral. They are kept in a shoebox in the kitchen, in order, more or less.
- **The harbour tide tables**: Printed every year by the harbourmaster; our copy is pinned inside the kitchen door. They are right about the tide and optimistic about the slip.
- **"A Day at the Lighthouse" (Cawdle school, 1966)**: A project by the village school's top class, with drawings. It remains the most accurate description of the lamp room ever written, and the only document on the Point with a theory about the chair.
